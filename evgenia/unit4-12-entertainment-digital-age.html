Listening: first watch

Correct answer: B. “Our attention faces many distractions, but there are ways to manage them.” A is too strong: the video says this doesn’t necessarily mean our attention spans are getting shorter. C is wrong: knocks on the door and our own passing thoughts also distract us.

Listening: second watch (checked against the video)

  1. What did the researchers measure? How long people stayed focused on one computer application before switching to another (study from 2016). On average it was 40 seconds, down from 75 seconds five years earlier.
  2. What can redirect our attention? Anything new, unexpected or emotionally charged: a knock on the door, a notification, even a passing thought.
  3. Why can multitasking make focusing more difficult? Some researchers think attention is a limited resource that is used up over time. Multitasking increases the demand for it, so it runs out faster.
  4. How can short breaks help? Even a very short break (getting a snack, zoning out) can reset this resource. When you’re learning something new, the brain replays what you just learned during breaks, which helps long-term memory.
  5. Practical ways to reduce distractions? Find a quiet environment, close extra tabs, block certain apps. The video also says it’s impossible to remove every distraction, so be kind to yourself.

Other strategies in the video, useful for extension: choose tasks that challenge you enough; reduce stress (exercise, chatting with friends, good sleep); a 15-minute walk outdoors; mindfulness meditation (focusing on breathing).

Myth or fact

  1. Fact. Our attention is pulled in more directions than ever before.
  2. Myth. Getting distracted is inevitable: the brain constantly scans for anything new or unexpected, which helped humans survive. The video says to be kind to yourself.
  3. Fact. Attention may be a limited resource; multitasking uses it up more quickly.
  4. Fact. A momentary break can reset attention and can help memory when learning.
  5. Myth. It’s impossible to remove every distraction; our own wandering minds are often the biggest one.

  2. “Some users also manage their screen time well and don’t feel that short videos affect their everyday lives.”

Language to watch for

  • Collocations: get distracted (not “be distract”), stay focused, take a break (not “make a break”), distracted by something.
  • Second conditional in the final challenge: “I would miss…”, “If I didn’t have…, I would…”. Recast, don’t stop the flow.
  • Use the speaking challenge for delayed correction: note 3–4 errors and review them at the end.

If the student speaks quickly: extension prompts

  • Warm-up: What did you do for fun ten years ago that you don’t do now?
  • Vocabulary: Which of the nine expressions describes you best this week?
  • Listening: Which of the four strategies would work best for you? Which wouldn’t work at all?
  • Myth or fact: Is it the user’s responsibility or the app’s responsibility when we spend too long scrolling?
  • Reading: Should schools teach students how to manage screen time?
  • Debates: Should apps show a warning after 30 minutes of scrolling? Is it rude to check your phone during a film with friends?
  • Final: If you designed a perfect evening with no screens, what would it look like?
